Introduction
         Much of the analysis done in support of a typical sounding rocket flight is for a
perfectly vertical launch. In general this is often a good approximation. But, of course,
real flights are never exactly vertical, and, so a means for adjusting vertical flight analysis
results to the nearly vertical flight of reality is needed. There are two main problems for
which these corrections are needed, dispersions and wind response.

Dispersion
        These notes document the derivation of the cell formulas used in Dispersion, an
approximate EXCEL based model of sounding rocket trajectory errors. Only the errors in
the most important events, apogee altitude and impact point location, are described. A
modified version of the classical Impulsive Burn Kepler Jump (IBKJ) model is used with
either a Galilean parabola or numerical derivatives, from, say, QROCKET, to map from
burnout to subsequent events. “Impulsive burn’ is shorthand for the assumption that the
powered flight trajectory arc is negligibly small compared to the coasting flight arc. This
is sometimes called the “gun shot” approximation.

        The event error statistics are assumed to be unbiased. This implies that the
predictive models have been suitably validated. Error sensitivities are assumed to be
based on linearized (small) perturbations in burnout velocity magnitude (energy errors)
and two components of rotation of the burnout velocity vector. Since energy and rotation
errors arise from different perturbing effects, they are assumed independent.
Furthermore, it can be shown (ref. (2)) that the two rotation errors are also independent.
Next, central limit theorem considerations ensure that the event errors have Gaussian
distributions. Finally, impact Circular Error Probable (CEP) values at specified capture
probabilities are estimated by an approximation due to Reid (ref. (3)).

       Finally, this is an extension & correction to the earlier report documented as ref.
(1).

                  var(h*) = (∂ h* / ∂ V)2 var(V) + (∂ h* / ∂ )2 var(

        Although it is not developed further here, the principal sources of burnout
velocity errors are weight errors, specific impulse errors and drag coefficient errors. The
principal sources of flight path angle errors are errors in measuring and forecasting the
turbulent wind field the rocket flies through, maladjustment of the launcher rail and the
misalignment of the thrust vector with respect to the rocket CG. These sources of error
are all mutually independent. Thus, the composite errors are just their RSS combination.

Event Statistics
        First, consider the distribution of impact points on the earth’s surface. Circular
Error Probable is defined as a circle within which a specified fraction of events occur. At
one point, the default fraction was implicitly taken to be 50%, but today it’s common to
say “95% CEP” meaning the circle containing 95% of the events. The CEP concept is
directly relevant as a description of impact point errors.
The probability obtained from a surface integral of a bivariate normal distribution over a
circular region of radius R mathematically defines the CEP. If the in-plane and cross-
plane standard deviations were the same, this would be a straightforward application of
the circular normal, or Rayleigh, probability distribution. The cumulative Rayleigh
distribution also applies to elliptical contours of constant probability density. If the two
standard deviations are equal, the Rayleigh result is
                          Pr(r ≤ R) = 1 – exp( – R2 / 2σ2), where

                         Pr = Probability the event is captured by the circle,
                         R = CEP at probability Pr, and
                         σ = Standard deviation of one component.
This relation can be solved for R to give

Wind Impact Displacement Algorithm
         These results can be used to design a sounding rocket trajectory to have the
desired impact location when flown through a known vector wind field. First,
decompose the vector winds into components lying in the desired trajectory plane and in
a plane normal to the trajectory plane. Next, consider the cross plane wind response.
Simulate the rocket response to cross plane winds starting from QE = 90o. Adjust the
initial QE until there is no cross rang impact point displacement. Do the same for the in
plane winds, and adjust its Q until the desired impact range is achieved. Combine the
QE adjustments in a vector sense to obtain the launcher QE and Azimuth required for a
successful launch.
