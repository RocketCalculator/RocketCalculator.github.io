Nozzle Thrust Coefficient — theory

Performance of a conical converging–diverging nozzle. Based on the one-dimensional isentropic flow relations of NACA Report 1135 and F. J. Malina’s correction for conical source flow, as used by C. P. Hoult (Rocket Science and Technology).

Open the calculator

Overview

A rocket motor nozzle is a de Laval, or converging–diverging, duct. Hot gas leaves the chamber at stagnation pressure p_c, reaches sound speed at the throat and keeps accelerating in the diverging section. The thrust it produces is written as

T=C_F\,p_c\,A_t

where A_t is the throat area and C_F is the thrust coefficient. C_F depends only on the gas, the nozzle shape and the ratio of ambient to chamber pressure. It measures how well the nozzle turns chamber pressure into thrust, independent of motor size. For \gamma=1.4 it runs from 1.27 for a sonic exit in vacuum to 1.81 for an infinitely long nozzle.

The calculator assumes the following:

  • the exhaust is a perfect gas with a constant ratio of specific heats \gamma;
  • the flow is steady and isentropic (no friction, heat loss or shock) from the chamber to the exit;
  • the throat is sonic, so the ambient pressure is below the throat pressure;
  • the diverging section is a straight cone of half angle \alpha.

One-dimensional isentropic flow

For isentropic flow of a perfect gas the pressure and the duct area at Mach number M are tied to the chamber (stagnation) state and to the sonic throat by

\frac{p}{p_c}=\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)^{-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}},\qquad \frac{A}{A_t}=\frac{1}{M}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}

At the throat M=1, so the throat pressure is p_t=p_c\,(2/(\gamma+1))^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}, about 0.53 p_c for \gamma=1.4. If the ambient pressure exceeds it, the throat cannot be sonic and the nozzle is only a venturi; the calculator refuses such a design.

The area relation has two roots for each area ratio. The supersonic one, with the expansion ratio \varepsilon=A_e/A_t, gives the exit Mach number M_e. The calculator finds it by bisection in \ln M to machine precision, then the exit pressure p_e from the pressure relation. Both depend only on \varepsilon and \gamma, not on the ambient pressure.

The jet momentum per unit chamber force is

C_{F,m}=\sqrt{\frac{2\gamma^2}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}}\left[1-\left(\frac{p_e}{p_c}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]}

The first factor under the root is the thrust coefficient of an infinitely long nozzle in vacuum. It is 1.81 for \gamma=1.4 and about 2.2 for \gamma=1.2.

Thrust coefficient and ideal expansion

The pressure difference between the exit and the surroundings acts on the exit area and adds

C_{F,p}=\frac{p_e-p_a}{p_c}\,\varepsilon,\qquad C_{F,1D}=C_{F,m}+C_{F,p}

A nozzle with p_e>p_a is under-expanded: a longer nozzle would extract more thrust. One with p_e<p_a is over-expanded: the surroundings push back on the exit. The one-dimensional thrust coefficient is largest when p_e=p_a. The calculator reports the expansion ratio that gives this ideal expansion at your ambient pressure.

Because p_e does not depend on p_a, thrust falls linearly as the ambient pressure rises. In vacuum (p_a=0) thrust is largest, and C_F keeps growing with \varepsilon towards the infinite-expansion limit. A nozzle sized for sea level therefore wastes performance at altitude, and an altitude nozzle is over-expanded at launch.

Malina divergence correction

In a conical nozzle the gas at the exit flows along rays from the cone apex, not parallel to the axis. Malina (1940) integrated the axial momentum of this source flow over a spherical exit surface. Only the fraction

\lambda=\frac{1+\cos\alpha}{2}

of the jet momentum is axial. The pressure term is unchanged, so the three-dimensional thrust coefficient is

C_F=\lambda\,C_{F,m}+\frac{p_e-p_a}{p_c}\,\varepsilon

A 15° cone gives \lambda=0.983, a loss of 1.7 % of the jet momentum. A wider cone is shorter and lighter but loses more; a narrower one is long and heavy. With the correction the best expansion ratio is slightly smaller than the ideal-expansion value.

The diverging cone has exit diameter d_e=d_t\sqrt\varepsilon and length L_d=(d_e-d_t)/(2\tan\alpha). The drawing shows it to scale; the converging section is drawn only for context and does not enter the calculation.

Flow separation

When a nozzle is strongly over-expanded, the pressure rise at the exit travels upstream through the wall boundary layer and the jet leaves the wall inside the nozzle. Separation sets in when p_e<0.4\,p_a. It is usually uneven round the circumference, so it produces side loads as well as a change in thrust.

The calculator warns when this happens and reports the expansion ratio at which it begins at your ambient pressure. The thrust coefficient is not valid once the jet has separated: use a smaller expansion ratio or a higher chamber pressure for a sea-level motor.

Verification

tests/nozzle.test.js checks the following:

  • every value of the RS&T example (\gamma=1.4, \varepsilon=6, 15°, 600 psia into 14.7 psia), giving M_e=3.3679, C_{F,1D}=1.4573 and C_F=1.4316;
  • area and pressure ratios against the NACA 1135 tables;
  • the sonic-exit limit C_F=(\gamma+1)(2/(\gamma+1))^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} and the infinite-expansion limit;
  • that the one-dimensional thrust coefficient peaks where p_e=p_a;
  • that the Malina factor reduces only the momentum term.

Notes on the method

  • Exit Mach number. It is solved to machine precision. The original example stops a fixed-end false-position iteration after 50 steps, which leaves the Mach number 6 × 10⁻¹⁰ too high; the thrust coefficients agree to 10⁻¹¹.
  • Ideal values. Friction, heat loss, two-phase flow and changes of gas composition along the nozzle are not modelled. Measured thrust coefficients are somewhat lower than these ideal values.
  • Exhaust properties. Use the ratio of specific heats of the actual exhaust. Air (1.4) is right only for cold-flow tests.
  • Added results. Thrust, vacuum values, the ideal-expansion ratio, the separation limit and the cone dimensions follow from the same relations.
