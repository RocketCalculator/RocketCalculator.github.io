Launcher Rail Deflection — theory

How stiff a launcher rail must be. Based on C. P. Hoult, Launcher Tipoff Deflection, 2010, written for the CSULB launcher described by F. Calderon et al., A New Launcher for University Sounding Rockets, 2011.

Overview

A university sounding rocket that reaches several kilometres can land far from its launcher unless the launcher is set for the winds. The CSULB launcher was built to be adjustable in azimuth (a turntable) and elevation (a scissor jack), transportable, and stiff: its rail rides on a ham-radio tower truss. Its requirements included a rail bending deflection of no more than 0.05° at a quadrant elevation of 80°; Hoult’s memo takes 0.1° as a good general upper bound.

The rail must also not rotate as a rigid body when the rocket launches, and its lugs should drop free together (a “zero tip-off” rail-and-lug design, first developed at NACA Wallops). This calculator addresses the bending.

The rail as a cantilever

The rail is cantilevered from its base. The bending is largest just before the rocket leaves, when the greatest moment acts over the greatest length, with the transverse load F at the tip:

EI\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=F(L-x)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{dy}{dx}(L)=\frac{FL^2}{2EI},\qquad y(L)=\frac{FL^3}{3EI}

where E is the Young’s modulus, I the second moment of area, x the distance from the base and L the rail length. The rocket takes about as long to run up the rail as the rail’s own bending period, so the load is effectively applied suddenly and the rail overshoots: the memo doubles the static slope (dynamic factor k_d=2). The tip slope is, in effect, a change in quadrant elevation at launch.

Solving for the length at which the slope reaches the allowance \theta_{max} gives the longest acceptable rail, L_{max}=\sqrt{2EI\theta_{max}/(k_dF)}.

Lateral loads

In the pitch plane the load is the weight component across the rail plus the thrust misalignment:

F_{pitch}=W\cos QE+n_\sigma T\sigma_\delta,\qquad F_{yaw}=n_\sigma T\sigma_\delta

The weight component is fixed; the misalignment is random, so it is taken at n_\sigma standard deviations (Ammons and Hoult give standard values; 0.1° is typical). For Gold Rush II (55 lb, 400 lb thrust, QE 80°) the weight component is 9.55 lb while a 3σ misalignment gives only 2.09 lb: the weight dominates.

Tower truss stiffness

A ham-radio tower is three steel tubes at the corners of an equilateral triangle of face width d, tied by welded zig-zag rods that add little bending stiffness. The neutral axis passes through the centroid, twice as far from the single tube as from the pair, so

I=\frac{Ad^2}{2},\qquad A=\pi Dt\quad\Rightarrow\quad I=\frac{\pi Dt\,d^2}{2}

for tubes of outside diameter D and wall t; the second moment about the perpendicular axis is the same, so pitch and yaw are equally stiff. The tip slope before the overshoot factor is then FL^2/(\pi EDt\,d^2). For other supports, enter I directly.

Verification

The tests check the memo’s Gold Rush II load comparison, the truss second moment against an exact parallel-axis sum in both planes, and the slope and deflection against numerical integration of the beam equation. They also reproduce the memo’s plot of tip angle against rail length (weight load only, with the factor 2): about 0.053° at 240 in and 0.21° at 480 in for QE 80°, and 0.027° at 240 in for QE 85°. This confirms the memo’s recommendation that a 20 ft rail keeps the tip-off error below about 0.1°.
