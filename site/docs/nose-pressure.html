Nose Pressure Distribution — theory

Surface pressures on a rocket nose at subsonic speed. Based on C. P. Hoult, Nose Pressure Distribution (rev. 4), 2012, after M. M. Munk, Note on the Pressure Distribution over the Hull of Elongated Airships with Circular Cross Section, NACA TN-192, 1924.

Why the nose pressure matters

A rolling rocket sees its body-fixed misalignments as torques at the roll frequency. When the roll rate nears the pitch frequency, the angle of attack grows. Above a modest angle of attack the boundary layer separates and sheds a pair of vortices along the body; when they reach the fins they induce a roll torque that can overpower the fin cant and lock the roll rate in. A smooth, continuously falling pressure along the body helps keep the boundary layer attached; an adverse gradient — pressure rising aft — encourages separation.

The circumferential pressure from cross flow, C_p=\sin^2\alpha(1-4\sin^2\theta), does not depend on the body shape. The longitudinal pressure does, and the nose designer controls it.

Munk airship theory

The flow round a body of revolution at zero angle of attack is represented by sources and sinks along its axis. A source of strength \sigma\,d\zeta at station \zeta induces at the point (z,r) the velocities

q_r=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{r\,\sigma\,d\zeta}{[(z-\zeta)^2+r^2]^{3/2}},\qquad q_z=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\frac{(z-\zeta)\,\sigma\,d\zeta}{[(z-\zeta)^2+r^2]^{3/2}}

For a slender body the flow from nearby sources is nearly radial, so flow tangency at the surface r_B(z) gives \sigma=2\pi U_\infty r_B\,dr_B/dz. Linearising Bernoulli’s equation, C_p\simeq-2q_z/U_\infty:

C_{p0}(z)=-\int\frac{(z-\zeta)\,r_B\,\frac{dr_B}{d\zeta}}{\left[(z-\zeta)^2+r_B^2\right]^{3/2}}\,d\zeta

Numerically the body radius is sampled at equal stations \Delta z apart and r\,dr/dz=r_2(r_3-r_1)/(z_3-z_1) from a parabola through three neighbouring stations. The sum runs over every station of body and wake. The method is poor near a blunt tip and close to a blunt base; elsewhere it gives a good picture of the pressure.

Closing the body: the wake

A source distribution must close, or the body would grow without end. Behind the base the flow forms a wake that narrows to a cylinder. Its diameter follows from two relations: the drag impulse equals the momentum lost by the air entering the cylindrical wake, and Bernoulli’s equation holds from the base (at the base pressure) to the wake, where the pressure has recovered. Solving them,

D_w=D\sqrt{\frac{C_D}{2\left(1-\sqrt{C_{Db}}\right)}}

where C_D is the forebody drag coefficient including the base and C_{Db} the base drag coefficient, both on the body cross-section. The wake converges as a cone of half angle \varepsilon; lacking better data, \varepsilon\approx0.1 rad.

Compressibility and the critical Mach number

The Kármán–Tsien rule scales the incompressible pressure to a subcritical Mach number:

C_p=\frac{C_{p0}}{\beta+\frac{C_{p0}}{2}(1-\beta)},\qquad\beta=\sqrt{1-M_\infty^2}

The critical Mach number is the lowest free-stream Mach number at which the surface flow somewhere reaches sonic speed. There the corrected minimum pressure equals the sonic pressure coefficient

C_p^*=\frac{2}{\gamma M^2}\left[\left(\frac{2+(\gamma-1)M^2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}-1\right]

and the calculator solves for that Mach number. Above it a supersonic pocket forms and the correction no longer applies. For the incompressible minimum of the memo’s example, C_{p0}=-0.0566, the critical Mach number is about 0.92.

Adverse pressure gradient

For most nose shapes the pressure coefficient bottoms out near −0.06 just behind the shoulder, where the air speeds up to round the corner, and then rises along the cylinder. The calculator reports the steepest rise per body diameter behind the minimum. Reshaping the nose — a gentler shoulder, a longer nose — cannot remove the dip, but the memo shows it can roughly halve the adverse gradient. Use the custom profile to explore faired shapes, and compare the standard shapes in the plot.

Axial force on the nose

The pressure on a slice of nose surface gives the axial force dF=2\pi r_Bp\,dr_B. With p=p_\infty+qC_p the hydrostatic part integrates to p_\infty times the base area (it cancels over a whole body but matters for internal loads) and the hydrodynamic part is

F=2\pi q\int_0^{L_n}C_p\,r_B\frac{dr_B}{dz}\,dz,\qquad C_A=\frac{F}{q\pi R^2}

The first element at a pointed tip contributes \pi qC_{p1}r_1^2. In subsonic flow the nose axial force can be negative — the suction on the shoulder pulls the nose forward.

Verification

The tests reproduce every station radius and every incompressible pressure coefficient of the original field tool’s ogive-cylinder (30 in ogive, 6.25 in body, 108 in long, with its wake). They check the wake diameter, the Kármán–Tsien and sonic pressure relations, a textbook critical Mach number (C_{p0,min}=-0.43\Rightarrow M_{cr}\approx0.72), and the convergence of the discrete sum to the exact line-source integral on a slender cone.

Notes on the method

  • Axial force. The field tool’s drag-area column omits the factor 2\pi on the body elements and sums only the first 15 in of the nose; the calculator integrates the memo’s formula over the whole nose.
  • Field point. As in the field tool, the distance from each source uses the body radius at the source station; this is the usual slender-body simplification.
  • Plume. The wake model is for a coasting (non-thrusting) rocket; a motor plume changes the flow behind the base.
