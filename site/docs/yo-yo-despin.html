Yo-Yo Despinner — theory

Despin weights for spin-stabilised stages and payloads. Based on J. V. Fedor, Theory and Design Curves for a Yo-Yo De-Spin Mechanism for Satellites, NASA TN D-708, 1961, as used in the RS&T yo-yo worksheet.

Overview

The yo-yo despinner was invented at JPL in the early 1960s and has been widely used since. Two weights sit on wires wrapped round the vehicle, at drum radius a. When the weights are released they fly outward and unwind the wires, carrying angular momentum away from the vehicle. Once fully unwound, each wire swings about its attachment point. When it lies along the radius the weights let go, at radius R=a+l. No propellant is used and the hardware is very simple.

Nothing outside the system acts on it, so its angular momentum and kinetic energy are conserved:

H=(I+ma^2)\,\omega_0,\qquad E=\tfrac12(I+ma^2)\,\omega_0^2

where I is the vehicle roll inertia and m the total despin mass.

Phase 1 — unwinding

With the unwound angle \varphi, the weight sits at the tangent point plus the unwound length a\varphi, so in the vehicle frame it traces an involute of the drum. The kinetic energy is \tfrac12I\dot\theta^2+\tfrac12ma^2[\dot\theta^2+\varphi^2(\dot\theta+\dot\varphi)^2]. Momentum and energy conservation then give Fedor’s classic results:

\varphi=\omega_0t,\qquad \omega=\omega_0\,\frac{c-\varphi^2}{c+\varphi^2},\qquad c=1+\frac{I}{ma^2}

Unwinding takes t_1=l/(a\omega_0). If the weights were released tangentially, the spin would reach zero at the wire length \lambda=a\sqrt c=\sqrt{I/m+a^2}.

The tension in each wire follows from the weight’s acceleration along the wire:

T_1=\frac m2\,a\omega_0^2\,\frac{4c\varphi(c-1)}{(c+\varphi^2)^2}

It peaks at \varphi^2=c/3, where T_{1,max}=\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{8}\,m\,a\omega_0^2\,(c-1)/\sqrt c. If the wire is shorter than that, the peak is at full unwinding.

Phase 2 — swing-out and radial release

At the instant the wire lies along the radius the weight moves purely tangentially, with speed u, at radius R. Conservation gives

(I+ma^2)\omega_0=I\omega_f+mRu,\qquad (I+ma^2)\omega_0^2=I\omega_f^2+mu^2

Eliminating u with r=\omega_f/\omega_0, D=I(1-r) and D^\prime=I(1-r^2) gives a quadratic for the despin mass:

(D+ma^2)^2=mR^2\,(D^\prime+ma^2)

Its coefficients have opposite signs, so there is exactly one positive root, which the calculator uses. Dropping the small terms in ma^2 gives Fedor’s closed form m\approx(1-r)I/[(1+r)R^2-a^2]. The two agree to within 0.1 % for usual designs and exactly when r=0.

The wire then turns at \dot\beta=(u-a\omega_f)/l, and the tension at release is

T_2=\frac m2\left(a\omega_f^2+l\dot\beta^2\right)

The swing-out is integrated in time from the full equations of motion, which gives the spin and tension histories and the release time. Over the swing the tension rises to this release value. It is continuous with the phase-1 tension at full unwinding.

Wire mass

Each wire of mass \mu l moves less than the weight at its end. It is counted as one third of its mass in the despin mass, so each weight is \tfrac12(m-\tfrac23\mu l). If the wires alone would supply more than the needed despin mass, the design is refused: use lighter or shorter wire.

Verification

tests/yoyo.test.js checks the following:

  • the closed forms against the worksheet example (2700 → 600 °/s, 17.2 ft wires on a 15 in drum);
  • that the exact despin mass conserves momentum and energy, and lies within 0.1 % of Fedor’s form;
  • that integrating both phases from rest ends at exactly the target spin, to 10⁻⁶;
  • that the simulated peak tensions match the closed forms;
  • that tension is continuous between the two phases.

Notes on the method

  • Despin mass. The exact positive root of the release quadratic is used. Fedor’s closed form is shown alongside.
  • Release tension. It comes from the exact release state. The worksheet’s simpler phase-2 estimate is about 20 % lower in the example and is shown only for comparison.
  • Model. Rigid vehicle, massless or lumped wire, no aerodynamic or gravity forces, and both weights released together.
